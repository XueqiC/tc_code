\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/fig1_mechanism.png}
\caption{The pipeline, illustrated end to end on an SQL-analytics
workload. (1)~One backward pass at the student's initialization reads each
of the $k$ example queries as the set of skills that student is missing
(named chips, e.g.\ \textsc{join}, \textsc{group by}). (2)~A sparse
dictionary over many teacher traces factors gradients into a library of
skill atoms; the atoms the $k$ queries activate (dashed) form the task
specification, while atoms such as \textsc{plot} fall outside it.
(3)~Teacher traces are scored by the atoms they supply: traces covering the
specification fill the token budget, a trace supplying \textsc{plot} is
rejected ($\lambda$ penalizes out-of-scope supply), and training stops
once the remaining in-specification demand is absorbed. (4)~At deployment
a conformal gate whose threshold is a calibration quantile (coverage
guaranteed rather than tuned) routes live requests: an in-specification
request reaches the student, an out-of-specification one is refused, and
both sides of the match carry certificates (in-task lower bound $0.93$,
off-task upper bound $0.25$; Section~\ref{sec:results}).}
\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}
Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarizes the pipeline. The method turns
distillation from a ranking problem into a bookkeeping problem. A short
warmup and one backward pass per example convert queries and teacher traces
into \emph{gradient fingerprints}, vectors that record what a student would
have to learn (Section~\ref{sec:fingerprints}). A sparse dictionary factors
these fingerprints into \emph{capability atoms}, a reusable vocabulary of
skills, in which the $k$ example queries become a \emph{demand ledger} and
every candidate trace becomes a \emph{supply schedule}
(Section~\ref{sec:atoms}). Selection then clears this market under the
token budget: each pick satisfies the largest remaining demand per token
and is charged against the ledger, so diminishing returns and domain
filtering arise from the accounting itself rather than from tuned
constants (Section~\ref{sec:clearing}). Finally, training is timed by behavior and the deployed student ships
with certified two-sided guarantees, because our experiments show that
gradient geometry reliably decides what to train on but not when a
capability has behaviorally arrived (Section~\ref{sec:behavior}). Every
threshold in the pipeline is either a statistical convention
($\alpha{=}0.1$, $90\%$ mass) or learned from the task's own calibration
data; no component is tuned per benchmark.

\subsection{Problem setting}\label{sec:setting}
A teacher agent $\pi_T$ acts by emitting executable code; a rollout on query
$q$ yields a trajectory $\tau(q)$ whose success is decided by an execution
verifier. A task domain is a distribution $P_\mathcal{T}$ over queries given
only through $k$ examples $Q_k \sim P_\mathcal{T}$, with $k$ between 5 and a
few hundred. From $(\pi_T, Q_k)$, a pool of teacher trajectories, a token
budget $b$, and a base student $\theta_0$, we must produce: (1) a boundary
predictor $\hat{B}(q) \in \{\text{in},\text{out}\}$ with distribution-free
coverage $\Pr_{q\sim P_\mathcal{T}}[\hat{B}(q)=\text{in}] \ge 1-\alpha$; and
(2) a student $\theta_S$ evaluated on the two-sided objective: coverage risk
$R_{\mathrm{cov}}$ (in-domain requests the student fails) and leakage $L$
(out-of-domain requests the student performs). We report
$(R_{\mathrm{cov}}, L)$ frontiers under matched token budgets and, following
the budget-to-target protocol, the minimal budget at which each method
reaches a target in-task level.

The method relies on two assumptions. \textbf{A1 (white-box student):} the
student's gradients are accessible; this is all that fingerprints, atoms,
and supply accounting consume. \textbf{A2 (success signal):} some check
decides whether an attempt succeeded; certificates consume only this binary
outcome, so an execution verifier, a unit test, or a judge are
interchangeable. Code-acting agents are the instantiation we evaluate, not
a requirement.

\subsection{Reading learning demands as gradient fingerprints}
\label{sec:fingerprints}
The pipeline begins by converting everything it is given, the $k$
example queries with their teacher solutions and the pool of candidate
traces, into a single common representation: one fingerprint vector per
example, read at the student initialization $\theta_0$. The central
representational choice is to describe an example not by its
text but by \emph{what the student would have to learn from it}: the
gradient that the example induces on the student. Identical query texts
solved in different styles are inseparable to any function of the text,
yet their gradients separate perfectly (hard-pair AUROC $1.000$ against
$0.687$ for trajectory embeddings, Section~\ref{sec:boundary}), because the
gradient records which parameters must change, not which words appear.
Concretely, we first run a brief warmup ($5\%$ of the pool for four
epochs with a low-rank adapter, following \citealp{xia2024less}), which
serves two purposes: the optimizer accumulates the second-moment statistics
required next, and the model absorbs surface formatting so that later
gradients reflect substantive skills. Each example then contributes one
backward pass; its adapter gradient is rescaled coordinate-wise by the
inverse root of the accumulated second moments, so that the vector measures
the step the optimizer would actually take rather than raw gradient
geometry, and is compressed by a fixed random projection. The projection is
lossless for our purposes: all downstream computations consume only inner
products, which random projections preserve to $(1\pm\varepsilon)$
\citep{johnson1984extensions}. Fingerprints are computed once and reused by
every later stage.

\subsection{A vocabulary of capability atoms}\label{sec:atoms}
The fingerprints just computed become the raw material of this stage,
which compresses them into a small vocabulary of skills and, in that
vocabulary, rewrites the task as a demand ledger and every candidate trace
as a supply schedule. The compression exploits a structural fact:
fingerprints of related examples are not arbitrary vectors; they reuse a
small set of recurring directions, the way recipes reuse a small set of
basic operations. Sparse dictionary learning recovers exactly this
structure. We solve
$\min_{D,C} \lVert X - CD\rVert_2^2 + \lambda\lVert C\rVert_1$
for a dictionary $D$ of $64$ atoms and sparse codes $C$, alternating a
lasso step (which few atoms explain this trace?) with a dictionary update.
The sparsity term is what makes atoms meaningful, and it is the design
choice that separates this component from PCA or SVD subspaces: a subspace
is invariant to rotations of its basis, so no individual principal
direction can be identified with a skill, whereas rotating a dictionary
destroys the sparsity of its codes. The only bases that survive the
$\ell_1$ penalty are those aligned with recurring, self-contained gradient
patterns, and those patterns are skills. Empirically the identification is
real: atom support separates fine-grained skills within one domain at
AUROC $0.906$ where the dense subspace reaches $0.615$, and the behavioral
change caused by distillation concentrates $85.5\%$ of its mass on $5$ of
$128$ atoms (Section~\ref{sec:atoms-results}).

The dictionary turns both sides of the problem into explicit accounts.
Encoding the $k$ queries (a lasso against the fixed dictionary) and
averaging their absolute codes yields the \emph{demand ledger}: how much
learning the task requires from each atom. The atoms holding $90\%$ of
this mass form the task's \emph{support}; encoding any trace yields its
\emph{supply schedule}, its per-token contribution to each atom. The same
calibration queries also provide an admission test with no tuned
threshold: a candidate trace is ranked among the held-out genuine queries
by subspace score, and candidates ranking below essentially all genuine
members (conformal $p$-value below $\alpha/2$) are excluded as outliers.

\subsection{Spending the budget: market-clearing selection}
\label{sec:clearing}
Selection now receives the two accounts prepared above, the demand
ledger scaled to the token budget and the supply schedule of every
admitted trace, and must return a training set. It does so by treating
tokens as currency and atoms as goods. The ledger opens
with each supported atom's demand converted into tokens; each round, the
selector picks the admitted trace with the largest value of
$\sum_a \min(\text{remaining demand}_a,\ \text{supply}_a(x)) / \text{tokens}(x)$
and immediately charges the ledger. Two properties follow from the
accounting alone. First, diminishing returns are endogenous: supply beyond
an atom's remaining demand counts zero, so a trace rich in an
almost-satisfied skill loses value automatically and the budget flows to
atoms with unsatisfied demand, with no decay constants. Second, domain filtering is
endogenous: a trace from a foreign domain supplies mostly atoms outside
the support, which never enter the sum, so it is deprioritized even when
the admission test cannot exclude it. This contrasts with influence-based
selection against a single mean target \citep{xia2024less}, which
collapses a multi-skill task onto one direction and, once that direction
is satisfied, converts every surplus token into out-of-scope capability
(off-task residue growing from $0$ to $0.44$ with budget on the SQL-scoped
task, Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\subsection{Certified training and deployment}
\label{sec:behavior}
The selected training set is all that enters the final stage, and what leaves
it is the complete deliverable: a trained student, its two-sided
certificates, and the runtime gate that will guard it. The stages above
use gradient geometry only to decide \emph{what} to train
on. Three pre-registered experiments show that the same geometry cannot
decide \emph{when} training has succeeded: instance-level gradient signals
fail to predict success, re-selecting against a partially trained
student's residual gradients selects noise, and geometric absorption
bottoms out roughly two orders of magnitude before behavioral competence
forms (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}). The training stage therefore hands
all judging to behavior. During supervised fine-tuning, a probe every $8$
steps measures the fraction of held-out calibration queries yielding
runnable solutions, and the best-scoring checkpoint is kept; this makes
aggressive small-budget training safe, because drift past the competence
peak is detected behaviorally and rolled back. Certification is the stage's centerpiece: the student is evaluated
behaviorally on both sides of the boundary, and the capability match is
stated as finite-sample Clopper--Pearson guarantees, a certified lower
bound on in-task coverage and a certified upper bound on off-task leakage,
so the deliverable is not a score but a bounded claim. At deployment, the same
fingerprint machinery gates live requests: the conformal threshold
calibrated in Section~\ref{sec:atoms} routes in-specification queries to
the student and refuses or escalates the rest, so the certificates
describe exactly the traffic the student will see.
