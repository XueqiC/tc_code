We record what each component of the method guarantees, and under which
assumptions, separating standard results from claims that our experiments
must earn.

\paragraph{Coverage of the boundary.}
For exchangeable in-domain queries, the split-conformal acceptance threshold
computed on $n_{\mathrm{cal}}$ calibration examples satisfies
$\Pr_{q\sim P_\mathcal{T}}[\hat{B}(q)=\text{in}] \ge 1-\alpha$ by the
standard quantile argument \citep{vovk2005algorithmic}; per-topic
calibration yields the conditional analogue \citep{gibbs2025conditional}.
This guarantee concerns acceptance of in-domain queries only. Rejection
power out of domain is an empirical quantity, and we report it as such.

\paragraph{Geometry preservation under sketching.}
The feature map composes two random projections: the adapter sketch, which
for a zero-initialized adapter equals $G A^{\!\top}$ with $A$ a fixed
Gaussian matrix, and a per-module Johnson--Lindenstrauss step
\citep{johnson1984extensions}. Pairwise inner products among a polynomial
number of gradients are preserved to $(1\pm\varepsilon)$ with the standard
logarithmic dimension dependence, which is the only property the method
consumes. A dimension-matched control (projecting to the embedding
baseline's dimension) changes hard-pair AUROC by less than $0.001$
(Section~\ref{sec:boundary}).

\paragraph{First-order capability prediction.}
Let $\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \sum_{x \in D} \nabla \ell(x;\theta_t)$.
A first-order expansion gives
$\Delta \ell(q) \approx -\eta \sum_{x\in D} \langle \nabla \ell(q;\theta),
\nabla \ell(x;\theta)\rangle$, so predicted loss movement is the kernel mass
the training set supplies to $q$. The approximation is exact in the lazy
regime and degrades with training length; rather than assuming the regime,
we measure the prediction quality (rank correlation $\rho=0.53$, $p=0.04$
across condition-domain cells at pilot scale) and pre-register thresholds
for its use (Appendix~\ref{app:limitations}). The same expansion explains
the observed sharpening mechanism: with cross-domain inner products
predominantly non-negative (fewer than 15\% negative), out-of-domain loss
increases arise from supply starvation combined with drift, not from
opposing gradients. \todo{formal statement with error term; cohort-level
validation on the sweep.}


\paragraph{Budget conservation and iso-performance efficiency.}
Write a token budget as $b = \alpha b + (1-\alpha)b$, where $\alpha$ is
the fraction of selected tokens whose trajectories supply the specified
atoms. To first order, coverage is driven by the on-specification supply
$\alpha b$ alone, so the budget required to reach a target in-task level
scales as $1/\alpha$: two selectors reaching the same in-task performance
differ in cost by the ratio of their on-specification fractions. The
off-specification remainder $(1-\alpha)b$ does not vanish; it is spent
installing out-of-scope capability, which is why a baseline's off-task
performance is the visible receipt of its wasted budget. Measured values
support the account: specification-guided selection runs at
$\alpha \approx 0.93$ against $\alpha \approx 0.29$ for random
selection, predicting a ${\sim}3.2\times$ budget ratio, and empirically
the specification-guided student at a 10k budget exceeds the random-diet
student at 40k on the 0.8B student (${\geq}4\times$;
Section~\ref{sec:distill}). We therefore report iso-performance budget
comparisons, with leakage presented as the destination of the budget other
selectors waste. \todo{tighten constants; multi-seed budget-to-target
table.}

\paragraph{Submodular selection.}
Selecting trajectories to maximize covered atom mass under a token budget is
monotone submodular maximization with a knapsack constraint, for which
cost-scaled greedy selection carries the usual $(1-1/e)$-style guarantee.
The ranked-prefix selection used in our experiments is the special case in
which per-item scores ignore diminishing returns; the gap between the two is
an ablation we report. \todo{exact statement and the ablation.}
