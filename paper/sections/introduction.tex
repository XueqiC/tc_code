\section{Introduction}
%% Storyline (v1.1): a text-only teacher says what to do, not what to
%% replace; imitation replaces wholesale; return-targeted distillation
%% fixes the teacher mass and chooses the replacement by the learning
%% return; evidence is priced by its marginal gain to the joint
%% update. House style: no em dashes, no colons, no prose

Enterprises increasingly deploy language-model agents for narrow,
recurring jobs, such as operating a fixed set of applications,
handling customer requests over database-backed tools, or automating
one workflow \citep{yao2023react, trivedi2024appworld, yao2024taubench,
shridhar2021alfworld}. Serving a frontier model for a single narrow
job is rarely economical, so practice converges on specializing a
small student for that job. What the deployer actually has is a few
dozen example queries from the intended workload, a task-level check
of whether an attempt succeeded, and metered query access to a strong
commercial teacher whose weights and logits are out of reach and whose
every emitted token is billed. The question this paper studies is how
to convert a limited teacher budget into the largest possible gain in
task success for the small student.

The standard answer is imitation. One purchases teacher
demonstrations and trains the student to reproduce them, a recipe that
underlies sequence-level knowledge distillation and its agentic
refinements \citep{kim2016sequence, mukherjee2023orca,
liu2025structured, kang2025distilling}. A demonstration from a
text-only teacher, however, answers only half of the question the
student faces. It says what the teacher would do at a state. It does
not say which of the student's existing behaviors at that state
should be replaced and which should be kept, and maximum-likelihood
training replaces them all at once, since its expected update descends
the divergence between the teacher and student behavior distributions
as a whole. On agent benchmarks the student already solves part of the
task, and the part that it solves is exactly what wholesale
replacement puts at risk. Preference-based and on-policy variants
change what is compared, but they still let the teacher text decide
both how much to move and what to move away from.

We separate the two decisions. Return-targeted distillation (RTD)
installs the teacher's text through a legal probability transfer: at
every supervised state a gate fixes, before anything is sampled, how
much teacher mass the state receives, and two fresh samples from the
student's own frozen snapshot define the behaviors that can give way.
Which of the two gives way is not read off the teacher text. It is
chosen by the student's own learning return, measured after a virtual
update on the same batch, so that the component shared by the two
student behaviors cancels and only their difference is decided.
Because several pieces of evidence enter one update, the choice is
made jointly, with an interaction term that charges evidence pushing
the update in a direction already taken. The same joint problem
prices acquisition. A candidate purchase is worth the marginal gain
it adds to what the evidence already owned can achieve in the shared
update space, including the teacher injection it brings, and
purchases are executed through a hard ledger that bills failed
completions at their recorded cost under a budget that is a cap
rather than an amount to be spent.

Our contributions are as follows. First, a problem. Budgeted
few-shot agent specialization asks, given a few-shot description of
an agent task and a budget on a per-token billed, text-only black-box
teacher, for a small model that maximizes task performance; the
budget is the single constraint and held-out task performance the
single criterion. Second, a mechanism. We show that a probability
transfer with fixed teacher mass and a return-chosen replacement
target admits an unbiased gradient estimator in which the shared
component of the student's competing behaviors cancels, and that at
equal teacher mass a small selective transfer improves the
finite-step return over the uniform one whenever the two behaviors
respond differently; the practical selection is a local surrogate
with an explicit redundancy term and estimation-error penalty, and
the same surrogate defines the value of evidence. Third, a protocol
and results. Under a common teacher budget cap per benchmark, with
the agent harness fixed and only the student backbone changing, we
compare RTD with implementation-faithful distillation baselines, and
we isolate the mechanism with paired controls that hold the purchased
data, the injection, and the source samples fixed and change only the
