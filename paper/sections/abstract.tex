\begin{abstract}
Language-model agents are increasingly deployed for narrow, well-defined
jobs, while the models that power them are trained to do everything.
Distilling a small, task-specific student is the economical answer, and in
practice the task is specified only implicitly, by a few dozen example
queries. Whether the result can be trusted then rests on two questions:
where the boundary of the specified task lies, and whether the agent built
on the distilled student covers exactly that boundary. We find that both
questions share one answer. The gradient that a teacher demonstration
induces at the student's initialization records what this student would
have to learn from it, and a sparse dictionary over such gradient
fingerprints resolves them into \emph{capability atoms}, recurring
learnable skills that a principal-subspace analysis provably cannot
identify. In this vocabulary the example queries become a demand ledger,
every candidate demonstration becomes a per-token supply schedule, and
distillation reduces to clearing a market: each selected trace satisfies
the largest remaining demand per token, so diminishing returns and
out-of-scope exclusion arise from the accounting itself, with no
benchmark-specific tuning. Because gradient geometry decides only what to
train on, competence is judged behaviorally: training keeps the checkpoint
that behaves best on held-out specification queries, and the deployed
agent ships with finite-sample certificates for both sides of the match.
On a controlled five-domain suite, atom-based students match the strongest
influence-based selection in-task at every budget while holding off-task
capability at zero where that baseline leaks up to 77\% of it, and never
spend budget outside the specification. The pipeline transfers unchanged to agent benchmarks.
\end{abstract}
