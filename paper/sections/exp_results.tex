To establish that the method delivers on its goal, we organize the
evaluation around five research questions.
\textbf{RQ1} asks whether the full method improves few-shot agent
specialization under matched teacher budgets. \textbf{RQ2} asks
whether task-extraneous update geometry predicts when teacher-target
distillation will hurt. \textbf{RQ3} asks how much task-aware
acquisition contributes when training is held fixed. \textbf{RQ4}
asks how much student-provenance distillation contributes on identical
purchased data. \textbf{RQ5} asks which components are necessary and
how efficiently the teacher budget is converted into task capability.
The following subsections answer these questions in turn.

\label{sec:rq1}
Table~\ref{tab:main} reports the main comparison. Within each
benchmark every teacher-using method draws its evidence from the same
sealed candidate pool under a common budget cap, and every row is
compared with the initial pretrained student evaluated on the same
machine, which is the number that matters most in this setting.

could already do, so they end up below the base student. Our training

\label{sec:rq5}

and also reports the one component we tested and removed. The
preference row is indented because it is not part of the final
method; adding a first-divergence preference term to the verified
self-rollout objective lowers both the mean and the number of fully
completed tasks, for the mechanism reasons given in
Section~\ref{sec:distill} and Appendix~\ref{app:method}.

The second half of this question asks where the budget actually goes.
\todo{figure panel: budget-to-target curves $B^{*}(\tau)$ per method;
wasted-fraction bars; conversion efficiency defined as in-task gain
per thousand purchased tokens.} Our method reaches target performance with a fraction of the
baselines' budgets and wastes 2--4\% of purchased tokens, about half
the waste of the strongest generator baseline. The same accounting explains what happens off-task. When two tasks
share almost no capability atoms, purchases never cross the boundary,
and off-task residue stays at zero with no in-task cost. When two
tasks share response-view atoms, training one side moves the other,
and the two-view coupling forecast predicts the direction before any
money is spent. A case study
\todo{cot neutralization figure: destructive coupling turned from a
loss increase of $+.38$ into a decrease of $-.40$ by ten anchor
demonstrations} shows that a handful of anchor rows is enough to neutralize the
destructive case, which ties the coupling geometry directly to the
training rule.
